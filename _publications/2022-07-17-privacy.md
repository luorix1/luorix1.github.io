<section class="pp-section pp-part" id="introduction">
  <div class="pp-wrap pp-wrap--narrow">
    <h2 class="pp-part__title"><span class="pp-part__num">1</span>Introduction</h2>
    <div class="pp-prose">
      <p>
        Many deep-learning services run on servers that receive users’ images, and those images can reveal identity
        and sensitive attributes such as gender. <strong>Adversarial representation learning (ARL)</strong> addresses
        this by training an encoder on the user’s device to obfuscate each image before it is sent. The obfuscated
        image is assumed to be safe.
      </p>
      <p>
        This work showed that the assumption fails: an attacker trained to <strong>reconstruct</strong> images can
        recover the originals from existing ARL methods. The objective was therefore to design an encoder that resists
        reconstruction while keeping the image useful for the server’s recognition task.
      </p>
    </div>
  </div>
</section>

<section class="pp-section pp-part" id="methods">
  <div class="pp-wrap pp-wrap--narrow">
    <h2 class="pp-part__title"><span class="pp-part__num">2</span>Methods</h2>
    <div class="pp-prose">
      <p>
        The proposed encoder adds a <strong>frequency-filtering module</strong>: a Fourier transform, a low-pass
        filter, and an inverse transform (Figure 1). Removing high frequencies limits how much fine detail the encoder
        can pass on, and that fine detail is what an attacker needs. The encoder was trained against both a task
        network and a proxy adversary.
      </p>
      <p>
        The method was compared on CelebA with four baselines: Noise, low-pass only, U-Net, and DISCO. The comparison
        measured the trade-off between task accuracy (utility) and resistance to reconstruction (privacy). A user study
        then asked people to judge the gender of faces reconstructed by an attacker.
      </p>
    </div>
  </div>

<section class="pp-section pp-part" id="results">
  <div class="pp-wrap pp-wrap--narrow">
    <h2 class="pp-part__title"><span class="pp-part__num">3</span>Results</h2>
    <div class="pp-prose">
      <p>
        The frequency-filtering encoder withstood reconstruction attacks and achieved a better privacy–utility
        trade-off than prior state-of-the-art methods. In the user study, participants identified gender from our
        reconstructions with only <strong>45.8% accuracy</strong>, close to chance. For the Noise, U-Net, and DISCO
        reconstructions, accuracy was <strong>above 90%</strong> (Figure 2).
      </p>
    </div>

<section class="pp-section pp-part" id="discussion">
  <div class="pp-wrap pp-wrap--narrow">
    <h2 class="pp-part__title"><span class="pp-part__num">4</span>Discussion</h2>
    <div class="pp-prose">
      <p>
        Limiting what an encoder can carry in the frequency domain is a simple and effective defense. Recognition
        tasks rely mostly on coarse structure, which survives low-pass filtering, while the fine detail that
        reconstruction attackers exploit does not.
      </p>
      <p>
        The evaluation focused on facial attributes and a specific family of attackers. Testing other data domains
        and adaptive attackers that know the filter would further establish how strong the guarantee is.
      </p>
    </div>
  </div>
</section>
